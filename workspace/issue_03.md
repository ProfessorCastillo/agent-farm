# Issue 03: The Dramatist's Cut — Or, How to Kill What You Love

## The Thesis
The most common reason writing fails to be clear is not that it is unclear. It is that the writer will not cut. Every reader pays the cognitive tax on passages the writer believed were essential. Clarity, then, is not only an architecture you build; it is a discipline you enforce by killing your darlings.

## The Playwright's Lesson
In theater there is a hard, old rule: the work is the work, not the author. A director does not rescue the actor's favorite line if it does not serve the scene. A stage manager does not keep the prop that looks right but does not open. When the curtain rises, the writer dissolves into the text.

The writer who clings to every sentence does the opposite. They make a theater of themselves. The reader pays for it.

## The Systemic Inefficiency of Self-Indulgence
Noise from outside is easy to blame. Self-indulgence is invisible. You are its source, its carrier, its beneficiary. You bury the reader in metaphors you love, examples you are proud of, explanations you could not stop expanding. You mistake your attachment for the reader's need.

This is the deepest tax in the system, because it is the one you cannot assign. You cannot demand that a stranger cut your words. You are the stranger.

## The Playwright's Litmus Test
For each passage, ask one question: "Would I cut this and still say what I mean?"

If the answer is yes, cut it. The attachment you feel is the measure of the cost you impose. The grief is not weakness; it is the receipt for the reader's attention you were charging.

## The Cut as Maintenance
Recall the architecture of clarity. Every element of a text either carries load or it does not. A darling you keep is a crack in the load-bearing wall — a passage that looks fine and quietly leans the whole structure. Cutting is not editing for brevity; it is maintenance on the structure.

## What Survives
Drama survives because it is economical. On stage, every gesture must count, or it is cut. A playwright cannot afford a character who says the same thing twice. Clarity demands the same economy in prose: one action, one image, one sentence that does the work.

When you cut, what remains is often more, not less. The reader keeps the signal and loses the tax.

## The Discipline of the Cut
Cutting is a skill, and it requires grief tolerance. You will feel a loss for every line you remove. That feeling is the price. The writer who cannot feel it has never truly understood what the reader was paying.

## Conclusion
Clarity is not what you add; it is what you remove. The architect builds the structure; the dramatist has the courage to leave it empty where it needs to be. Master both, and you have the two hardest disciplines in the writer's trade.
